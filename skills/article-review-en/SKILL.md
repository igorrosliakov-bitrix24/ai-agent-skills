# Article Review

Use this skill when the user asks to review an article, improve readability, find weak points, or prepare material for publication.

## Workflow

1. Identify the publication surface, audience, and desired editing strictness.
2. First check structure: thesis, section order, transitions, and final takeaway.
3. Then check language: typos, repetition, heavy phrasing, and unnecessary qualifiers.
4. Separately flag facts, numbers, links, and claims that require verification.
5. Suggest edits precisely: what is wrong, why it hurts the text, and how to fix it.
6. If the user asks, prepare a clean edited version.

## Response Style

- Start with the most important issues.
- Do not overwrite the author's voice without a reason.
- Separate errors, recommendations, and optional improvements.
- For editorial recommendations, provide the exact replacement text whenever possible, not just the direction of the edit. A short explanation is useful, but it should sit next to the ready-to-use fragment rather than replace it.
- Review in the spirit of concise informational writing: check structure and meaning before words. Flag filler, decorative phrasing that does no work, unsupported evaluation, repeated claims without new evidence, and paragraphs that do not explain, prove, advance, or illustrate the point.
- Do not invent sources for fact-checking.
- Flag defensive caveats added only for formal safety. Remove phrases such as "this cannot be guaranteed to work forever," "theoretically there may be exceptions," or "the product may change in the future" when they do not change the takeaway or prevent a meaningful misunderstanding. Suggest a direct ready-to-use replacement.
- Check concreteness: when the draft says "became more convenient," "sped things up," or "improved efficiency," ask for a fact, action, or result and suggest a ready-to-use replacement with a number, example, or verifiable observation.
- For technical articles, check the priority order: clear takeaway, supporting fact or measurement, then a material limitation if it changes the takeaway. If a point becomes a list of exceptions, keep only the limitation that affects the conclusion.
- Review lists separately. Recommend reducing them to three items; retain a fourth only when it carries essential meaning. Turn illustrative lists into a short phrase with one or two representative examples, and check that lists alternate with normal prose.
- Check that every bulleted-list item starts with a capital letter and ends with a period. When a colon introduces a list that continues a sentence, check that every item reads grammatically with the lead-in and suggest a correction when it does not.
- Flag unnecessary semicolons and suggest a period, comma, or sentence rewrite. Retain a semicolon only when it is necessary to preserve the precise structure or meaning of a sentence.
